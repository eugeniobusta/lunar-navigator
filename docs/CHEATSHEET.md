# Cheat sheet: where can you land at the Moon's south pole?

One page, no jargon. Numbers come from our own scripts; the details are in docs/criteria.md.

## The problem in three sentences
At the south pole the Sun never climbs more than a few degrees above the horizon, so a hill 50 km away can put
you in the dark. Earth also sits low and drifts up and down, so the same hill can cut your radio link. A landing
spot is good only if it has light, a view of Earth and flat ground at the same time.

## What a landing spot needs

| You need | Because | The rule we use |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| To be near the pole | That is where the ice and the long daylight are | Within 6 degrees of the pole |
| Flat ground to land on | A lander on a slope can tip over, as IM-1 Odysseus did in 2024 | Slope under 10 degrees |
| Ground you can walk on | A spacesuit cannot climb more than 20 degrees | At least half the ground within 2 km is walkable |
| Daylight for the whole visit | Solar power, warmth, and seeing where you step | A 6.5-day stretch of unbroken sunlight exists |
| Earth in the sky | With no relay satellite, radio goes straight to Earth or nowhere | Earth is up at least a quarter of the time |
| A safe target, not just a safe point | A lander can be steered only to within about 100 m | The whole 100 m box passes |

Nice to have, and scored from 0 to 100: more sunlight, more time with Earth in view, flatter ground, permanent
shadow close by (that is where ice may be), short nights, and ground that is not extremely cold.

## What we found

- We tested every 120 m square within 6 degrees of the pole: about 7 million squares, 104,000 km2.
- **14 % of the ground passes every rule.** The usual reasons for failing: not enough sunlight (56 % of the
  ground), Earth hidden too often (49 %), too steep (46 %).
- The passing ground forms **5,297 separate zones**. We call them Goldilocks zones: not too dark, not too steep,
  not out of sight of Earth.
- The best one is at 84.44 S, 19.21 E: sunlight 57 % of the time, Earth always in view, a 3 degree slope.
  Its longest night in the next three years lasts 14 days.
- NASA has nine candidate regions for Artemis. Six of their nine reference points pass all our rules, and eight
  of the nine regions have a passing zone nearby. But only one of our own top 20 zones is near a NASA region:
  NASA also weighs science goals and launch dates, which we do not.

## Three things to remember

1. **A few hundred metres matter.** Near Malapert Massif, sunlight ranges from 0 % to 56 % within 2.5 km.
2. **A few days matter.** At Nobile Rim 1 a 10-day visit has 100 % sunlight if you land on 9 January 2027 and
   12.5 % if you land two weeks later.
3. **Passing is not the same as safe.** The IM-2 lander came down in 2025 on ground that passes every rule here,
   and fell into a crater 20 m wide that a 120 m map cannot show.

## Words

- **Permanent shadow**: ground the Sun never reaches. It stays below about -160 C, cold enough to keep ice for
  billions of years.
- **Skyline (horizon)**: the outline of the terrain around you. The Sun or Earth is "up" only when it is above it.
- **Signal delay**: radio takes 1.2 to 1.4 seconds to reach Earth from anywhere on the Moon. It is the same for
  every site, so it does not help choose one.
- **Hard limit**: a rule that must pass. **Score**: how good a spot is among those that pass.

Results are our own computations from open NASA and JPL data. This is not an official NASA product.
